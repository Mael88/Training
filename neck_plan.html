Why This Plan Exists

The situation: recurring tension headaches with an active cervical hernia. Headaches appear at the end of the day, not after morning training — and they track with desk/PC hours more than with operating days.

What that means: training is probably not the trigger. Sustained static neck flexion at a desk is the prime suspect; training was lowering the threshold rather than causing the problem. The OR is uncomfortable but bounded — you move between cases. Desk work is hours of the head held forward with no position change, which fatigues the deep neck flexors and hands the job to the upper traps. That is the same substitution pattern showing up as left trapezius overactivation in your striking.

The strategy: fix the desk exposure first, lower the trapezius tension that builds up from training, desk and stress, raise the neck's tolerance with daily prehab and deep neck flexor strength, and use the log to confirm the pattern.

Desk & Screen Setup · highest-yield change in this plan

Workstation Priority

  • Top of the screen at eye level — if your chin drops to read, the setup is wrong.
  • Screen about an arm's length away — closer pulls the head forward.
  • No laptop-only work — external monitor, or a laptop stand plus separate keyboard.
  • Forearms supported — unsupported arms hang from the traps all day.
  • Documents and second screens in front, not to the side — sustained rotation is worse than sustained flexion.
  • Keyboard and mouse close — elbows by your sides, wrists straight, hands at or just below elbow level.
  • Headset for long calls; phone up to your eyes — not your head down to the phone.
Mayo Clinic: office ergonomics guide

Break the static load Triggers, not timers

A timer every 30–45 min doesn't stick, so tie a 10-second reset to things that already happen: finishing a case, closing a patient file, sitting back down at the desk. One shrug-and-drop (shrug 3 sec, let go completely) plus one slow belly breath.

Backstop: Garmin's Move Alert can buzz you after long sitting. The problem is duration of a fixed position, not intensity — any interruption helps.

Daily Prehab · every day, not just training days · ~5 min

Morning routine Daily

  • Chin tucks — 2×10, slow and controlled. Seated or standing.
  • Cervical rotations — 5 each side, pain-free range only.
  • Lateral neck stretch — 20–30 sec each side, gravity only.
  • Scapular retractions — 2×15. Unloads the cervical spine.
  • Shoulder rolls — 10 forward, 10 backward.
Second full dose on heavy desk days. Midday or as soon as you finish a long screen block. The trigger-based resets during the day are separate.
Trap Tension Plan · every evening · ~5 min

Not lactic acid — that clears within about an hour. It's muscle tone that builds up from training, desk and theatre posture and stress, then drives headaches later in the day. The routine below releases it directly, attached to something you already do so it needs no new habit.

Evening routine — straight after your shower Daily

  • Heat — 2–3 min. Hot water on the upper traps in the shower, or a heat pack for 15 min on the sofa.
  • Massage gun or ball — 1–2 min per side. Gun: low setting, slow sweeps over the meaty part of the upper trap, 10–15 sec max on a tender spot. Ball: tennis or lacrosse ball between the trap and a wall, about 5/10 pressure, breathe. Stay off the spine, the bony points and the front and side of the neck. ▶ Massage gun (Blackroll)▶ Ball release (Penticton Physio)▶ Lacrosse ball video
  • Upper trap stretch — 2×30 sec per side. Sit on the hand of the side you stretch, let the ear drop toward the opposite shoulder. Gravity only — skip the hand pull some videos show. ▶ Ask Doctor Jo
  • Levator stretch — 30 sec per side. Nose toward the opposite armpit, head drops slightly, gravity only. ▶ Physical therapist demo
  • Belly breathing lying down — 1 min. One hand on the chest, one on the belly; 4 sec in so the belly hand rises, 6 sec out. Upper-chest breathing recruits the traps and scalenes. ▶ [P]rehab demo

Lower traps — 2–3× per week In the warm-up

  • Wall slides, shoulder blades down — 2×10. Forearms on the wall, slide up slowly while pulling the shoulder blades down and away from the ears; stop where the shoulders start to shrug. Builds the lower traps so the upper ones stop doing all the work. ▶ Mass General Sports PT▶ SOS Physio (photos)

In training — don't add to it

  • Pull-ups and dips: when the shoulders start shrugging toward the ears, the set is over.
  • Bag: shoulder blades down on every punch; drop the shoulders between combinations.
  • Cool-down: never skip the trap stretches and breathing.

The nap — when the office is ready

10–20 min after lunch helps stress and sleep debt. Lie flat, or recline with the head supported — sleeping slumped with the head dropping forward loads the neck. On days without a nap, 5 min flat on your back with belly breathing still takes the head's weight off the traps.

Deep Neck Flexor Program

The deep flexors (longus colli, longus capitis) are the stabilizing "core" of the cervical spine. When they fatigue or weaken, the superficial muscles — SCM and upper traps — take over. That substitution is exactly what hours at a desk produce, and it is the same pattern as your left trap overactivation when striking. Training the DNFs is the best-evidenced intervention for reducing cervicogenic and tension-type headache frequency. It is threshold-raising work: boring, low-intensity, effective.

Stage 1 — Motor Control Baseline

Chin tucks, 2×10 daily (already in the routine above). A pure "nod" — the back of the head slides up, the chin drops slightly. No forward flexion, no SCM bulging.

Move to Stage 2 when: tucks feel effortless and symptom-free. You are there now — start Stage 2 in week 7.

Stage 2 — Craniocervical Flexion Holds Start week 7

Setup: lying on your back, knees bent, tongue on the roof of the mouth, jaw relaxed.

Movement: gentle nod as if saying "yes" — the smallest movement that slightly flattens the curve of the neck. Hold 10 seconds, breathe normally, release.

Dose: start 5×10 sec, build to 10×10 sec. Daily. Rest ~10 sec between holds.

The test: two fingers lightly on the SCM at the side of the neck. If it fires hard, the effort is too high — make the nod smaller. The work must stay deep.

Move to Stage 3 when: 10×10 sec is easy with zero SCM substitution and no symptoms, for at least a week.

Stage 3 — Low-Load Endurance ~Wk 2–3 of new cycle

  • Head lift with tuck — supine, chin tucked, lift the head 1 cm off the floor. Hold 5–10 sec, 3×5. The tuck must not release during the lift.
  • Resisted isometrics — hand against forehead, back of head, then each side. Push gently, neck does not move. 3×10 sec each direction.

3–4 days/week alongside the daily prehab. Replaces the Stage 2 holds on the days it is done.

Maintain long-term: once headache frequency drops, 2×/week keeps the adaptation.

The correlation to watch is headache vs desk hours, not headache vs training. If a 6-hour desk day reliably produces a headache and a training day without desk work does not, the lever is the workstation and the micro-breaks — and upper-body training can be rebuilt faster than planned.
Stop Criteria — Non-Negotiable
During a training week: headache during or immediately after a session, or cervical status "flare-up" → cut the week short and return to rest. No mid-week negotiation.
Any time: arm pain, numbness, tingling or weakness radiating below the shoulder → stop training and get it assessed. That is nerve territory, not muscle tension.
After 4+ weeks of the plan: if headaches persist despite the desk changes, the trap routine, reduced load and daily prehab → physio for manual therapy and a supervised rehab block. Not to fix the hernia — to manage the muscular driver.
